# Discovery calls — the first conversation

The call has one job: the person on the other end ends it thinking "we should probably be doing this." Not "this person is looking for work."

Order matters. If the founder decides mid-call that the conversation was a pitch in disguise, everything earlier gets re-read as manipulation and weeks of trust vanish in a sentence.

## Frame intent transparently

Advice to hide the intent — never mention wanting to do the work — technically functions but reads as concealment, and sophisticated operators clock it. A cleaner frame: transparent intent, deferred ask.

> "Honestly, I have been looking at this market as somewhere I might want to work, directly or on a project basis. Not asking about that today — I want to know whether the thing I noticed is a real gap or whether I am missing context. If it is a real gap and you are the right person to talk about it later, I will come back."

Same outcome (no pitch on call one) without the theatre. Founders relax faster with transparent framing.

## Structure, roughly timed

**Zero to two minutes: why this market.** Real reason for interest, plus enough evidence of homework that they relax. Name what was looked at — competitors, funnel, numbers estimated.

**Two to eight minutes: present the pattern.** What is happening in the market, with specifics. Who is doing it. How it appears to be working. Why it fits this type of business. The observation, not the prescription.

**Eight to ten minutes: the pivotal question.** Ask with genuine curiosity why they are not doing it. They may know something that kills the idea.

> "Most of your closest competitors run this. You are not, and I assume that is deliberate — is there a reason it does not work for your model?"

**Ten to twenty-five minutes: listen.** The answer routes everything after.

- "Tried it, did not work" — find out exactly how. Who ran it, what budget, over what period, what was measured. Failed attempts are usually abandoned experiments, not verdicts. But if it genuinely failed for a structural reason, update and believe them. Arguing with data they have and the user does not ends credibility.
- "Our model is different because…" — often partially true. Ask what would have to be true for it to work.
- "We have thought about it, no time" — the best available answer. Keep going.
- "Someone is on it" — ask how it is going. Sometimes "someone" is a part-time contractor with no ownership, which is still an opening. Sometimes it is closed — accept it and ask what they are actually short of.

**Twenty-five to thirty minutes: walk through the money.** Their product, their numbers, the mechanism, the rough upside. Stay in "if this works for you" framing. Show two competitor cases.

**Thirty minutes: close and stop.** Thank them, offer the research write-up, ask the one question that keeps the door open:

> "Who else in this market should I be talking to?"

End the call.

## What not to say

- "I could do this for you." The most common way this method fails.
- "Are you hiring?"
- Anything that reads as criticism of their team's speed, size, or judgment. The gap is opportunity, not evidence of failure.
- Claims the user cannot back. Founders check, often with people the user knows.
- Overconfident certainty about a market the founder has lived in for years. Confidence in the method, humility about their specifics.

## Why stopping works

After a good call, the founder is thinking about the opportunity, and often realizes they have nobody to run it or no time to run it themselves. At that moment they already know exactly one person who has researched this problem, understands their business, and has done it before.

That is a stronger position than being the person who asked for work in minute nine. Often the founder raises it themselves, which changes the negotiation.

## When they ask on-call

If they say "would you want to work on this?" — that is the desired outcome. Do not oversell.

> "Yes, I would want to test it. I would rather prove it than get hired on a promise — let me put together what a small trial would look like and you can see if it is worth doing."

Then move to `pilot-design.md`.

## After the call

Send a note within twenty-four hours: two or three lines of thanks, the competitor cases and any numbers, nothing asked for. Concrete and useful — reinforces the peer frame rather than reopening a pitch.

Then leave it alone four to seven days. If they do not come back, the pilot workstream has a soft reopener.

## Output

A one-page call plan sized to be glanced at mid-call: opening, the pattern with two to three evidence points, the why-not question in the founder's vocabulary, branch responses for the four common answers, the one line not to say, the closing question.
